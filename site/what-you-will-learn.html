Every lesson

How a two-hour lesson works

Two hours gives you time to settle in, practise properly and finish on something you have got right.

  1. Pick-up

    Seyma collects you from home, work, school or college, in a dual-control automatic car.

  2. Today’s goal

    You look back at your last lesson and agree what to work on today.

  3. Practice

    Plenty of time on roads that suit the skill, starting on quieter roads while it is new.

  4. Look back

    You talk through how it went and what comes next, then Seyma drops you back.

Stage 1 of 5

First lessons: the car, moving off and stopping

Getting comfortable with the car, so the controls become second nature before you meet busier roads.

  • Cockpit checks

    Before you start the engine: doors shut, then your seat, head restraint and steering wheel, your seatbelt, and the mirrors last. The parking brake is on and the gear selector is in P or N.

  • Controls and instruments

    In an automatic, your right foot works both the accelerator and the brake. You learn the gear selector (P, R, N and D), the parking brake, indicators, lights, wipers and demisters, and what the dashboard warning lights mean, until you can use them without looking.

  • Moving off and stopping

    On the level, on a hill and from behind a parked car, using the mirrors–signal–manoeuvre (MSM) routine and checking your blind spots. Then stopping smoothly in a place that is safe and legal.

    In the test: a normal stop at the side of the road, pulling out from behind a parked car, and a hill start.

  • Safety checks

    Fuel, lights, oil, water, electrics and rubber (the tyres), remembered as FLOWER, and checking the brakes and steering before you set off.

    In the test: one ‘tell me’ question before you drive and one ‘show me’ question while you drive. All 21 questions, with answers.

  • Legal responsibilities

    How your health, eyesight, tiredness, alcohol, drugs and mobile phones affect your driving, and how to check that a car is taxed, insured, has an MOT if it needs one, and is safe to drive.

  • Security

    Keeping yourself and the car safe: valuables out of sight, a secure place to park, especially at night, and letting someone know where you are going.

Stage 2 of 5

Roads and junctions

Reading the road around you, keeping the right position and speed, and turning in and out of side roads safely.

  • Safe positioning

    The right position for the road and the traffic: in the lane you need in good time, around bends and on one-way streets, with enough room when you pass parked cars and cyclists.

  • Mirrors

    When and why to check each mirror, what each one shows you, where your blind spots are, and acting on what you see.

  • Signals

    Clear signals at the right time, knowing when a signal is not needed, and reading other people’s: brake lights, hazard lights, arm signals and school crossing patrols.

  • Use of speed

    The speed limits, adjusting your speed to the road, the weather and the traffic, slowing down where people are walking, and keeping a safe gap behind the car in front.

  • Junctions

    T-junctions, crossroads and unmarked junctions: turning in, emerging, who has priority, and the position–speed–look (PSL) routine as you approach.

  • Pedestrian crossings

    Zebra crossings, crossings with lights, split crossings and school crossing patrols: spotting them early, approaching at the right speed and knowing when to stop.

Stage 3 of 5

Busier roads and roundabouts

More traffic, higher speeds and more to plan for.

  • Roundabouts

    Mini, normal, multiple and traffic-light roundabouts: choosing your lane, giving way, and signalling as you leave.

  • Other traffic

    Meeting oncoming traffic past parked cars and on narrow roads, turning right across traffic, and overtaking only when it is legal and safe.

  • Anticipation and planning

    Spotting hazards early from the clues around you and deciding in good time what to do: cyclists, motorcyclists, buses and lorries, pedestrians, animals and emergency vehicles.

    In the theory test: the hazard perception part tests this skill.

  • Dual carriageways

    Joining from a slip road, choosing the right lane, the speed limits, turning right off a dual carriageway, and what to do if you break down.

  • Country roads

    Bends, hedges and hills that hide what is ahead, the national speed limit (a limit, not a target), passing places, horse riders, cyclists and farm vehicles.

Stage 4 of 5

Manoeuvres and the emergency stop

Slow, precise control of the car, looking all around as you go.

  • Parking

    Four ways to park: forward bay parking (driving in, reversing out), reverse bay parking (reversing in, driving out), parallel reverse parking (reversing into the space behind a car already parked at the side of the road), and pulling up on the right-hand side of the road, reversing about two car lengths and rejoining the traffic. Also where you are allowed to park.

    In the test: you will be asked to do one of the four.

  • Reversing

    Reversing slowly and smoothly under full control, in a straight line and into a space. The car’s camera and sensors help, but you learn to look all around yourself.

  • Turning the car around

    Using a roundabout or a side road, or turning in the road when there is no other way, judging the width of the road without touching the kerb.

    Not part of the test, but it is still taught in lessons.

  • Emergency stop

    Stopping as quickly as you can while keeping control, what ABS does and does not do, and moving off again safely. Reading the road well means you should rarely need one.

    In the test: you might be asked to do one.

Stage 5 of 5

Test ready

Driving on your own, in any conditions, the way you will once you have passed.

  • Independent driving and sat nav

    Following a sat nav or road signs without turn-by-turn directions, planning ahead so you never have to decide late.

    In the test: 20 to 35 minutes of it. The examiner sets up the sat nav, and taking a wrong turning is not a fault.

  • Driving in the dark

    Which lights to use and when, judging speed and distance at night, and parking safely in the dark.

  • Weather conditions

    Rain, fog, low sun, wind, ice and snow: how each one changes what you can see, your speed and your stopping distance, and which lights to use.

  • Passengers and loads

    Seatbelt rules for adults and children, never a rear-facing baby seat in front of an airbag, and luggage that is secure and evenly loaded.

  • Fuel-efficient driving

    Planning ahead and easing off early instead of braking hard, gentle acceleration and the right tyre pressures: cheaper and cleaner driving.

Nearly ready? A mock test runs like the real one, so you know what to expect on the day. In Seyma’s experience, pupils who pass five of her mock tests are very likely to pass the real test.

Test day

The driving test at a glance

The practical test is the same in an automatic. You drive for about 35 minutes.

You pass if you make no serious or dangerous faults and no more than 15 driving faults.

You can take the test in Seyma’s car: she picks you up, gives you a warm-up drive and takes you home afterwards. Compare pass rates at the test centres near Chingford.

  1. An eyesight check: reading a number plate from 20 metres.
  2. A ‘tell me’ question before you set off.
  3. Driving on different roads and in different traffic, with a normal stop, pulling out from behind a parked car and a hill start.
  4. A ‘show me’ question while you are driving.
  5. 20 to 35 minutes of independent driving, following a sat nav or road signs.
  6. One of the four parking manoeuvres. You might also be asked to do an emergency stop.

Alongside your lessons

The theory test and practice

You can start lessons before your theory test, but you must pass it before you can book your driving test. You take both parts on the same day and need to pass both.

Multiple-choice questions

50 questions in 57 minutes, and you need 43 right to pass. They come from The Highway Code, Know Your Traffic Signs and Driving: the essential skills. GOV.UK has a free practice test.

Hazard perception

14 video clips. You score up to 5 points for each developing hazard you spot, and you need 44 out of 75 to pass.

Practice between lessons

Extra driving with family or friends helps, as long as they meet the rules for supervising a learner and you are insured to drive the car.

Your progress

Five levels for every skill

DVSA’s driver’s record marks each skill at one of five levels, from the first try to complete confidence.

  1. Introduced

    You have started on it and can follow instructions.

  2. Helped

    You are getting better but still need some help.

  3. Prompted

    You need the odd reminder, especially somewhere new.

  4. Independent

    You do it well every time, on your own.

  5. Reflection

    You can explain what would have happened if you had done it differently, and why the skill keeps you safe.

Ready for your test? You are close when you can handle every skill on this page on your own, every time, without help or reminders.
